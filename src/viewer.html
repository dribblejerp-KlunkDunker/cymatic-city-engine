    How it works

    Frequency to pattern. The drive tone shakes a plate. Each plate has natural modes at fixed frequencies: Bessel zeros for the disk membrane (f ∝ jmn), m² + n² for the square Chladni plate, √(l(l+1)) for the sphere. The engine sums the modes near your frequency, weighted by a damped resonance of sharpness Q, the way a real driven plate responds.

    Pattern to plan. Sand on a Chladni plate settles on the nodal lines, where the plate does not move. Those lines become boulevards. Contour rings follow the amplitude, and gradient streets run from each nodal line up to the crest, so a ring-and-spoke plan grows around every antinode. Amplitude sets density and height; the sign of the wave splits the city into alternating districts.

    Realism rules. Streets aim for UN-Habitat's 18 km of street per km². Every building keeps a setback from every road and sits within walking reach of a street. Heights stay under the material's self-weight limit σ/(ρ·g·3) and a slenderness limit that eases at low gravity. Population assumes 45 m² per resident and 15 m² per worker.

    What the frequency does not mean. The standing-wave patterns are real physics; turning them into a city is a design choice. No study shows 432 Hz, Solfeggio tones or the Schumann resonance make a place healthier or more harmonious, so the engine treats every frequency alike. Some Neolithic chambers do resonate near 95–120 Hz; whether that was intended is still debated.
