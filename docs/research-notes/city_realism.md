## 1. Procedural city generation methods, and which suit roads that follow a scalar field's contour lines

### Takeaway
Chen et al. 2008 tensor-field street modeling is the best fit for roads that follow contour lines of a scalar field. Its paper explicitly builds a tensor field from a height field so that the minor eigenvector aligns with the gradient, so the major hyperstreamlines run along contours. Parish & Müller's L-system can also respond to a height map, but it works by local growth rules and has no direct equivalent. Galin et al. 2010 (weighted anisotropic shortest paths) is the other option when you want a handful of cost-optimal roads instead of a full street grid.

### Cited Findings
- Parish & Müller 2001 takes image maps (land/water boundaries, population density) as input, extends L-systems with "global goals and local constraints", generates highways and streets, subdivides land into lots, and builds geometry on each lot. — [SIGGRAPH History Archive](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=116923)
- In that system the global goals are street patterns and population density, the local constraints are land, water and park boundaries, elevation and street crossings. Lots are made by recursively subdividing along each lot's longest edge. Buildings are generated by parametric stochastic L-systems, one per lot. — [Purdue SIGGRAPH 2011 course notes, Urban Layouts](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/urban/sg_2011_course/umc_SG11_02_urban_layouts.pdf)
- Parish & Müller used a height map as an extra layout constraint and generated building geometry per plot as a function of population. — [Lechner et al., Procedural city modeling (arXiv 2507.18899)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18899)
- Chen et al. 2008 uses a second-order symmetric tensor field whose two eigenvector families give two orthogonal street families. Streets are hyperstreamlines. Designers combine basis fields with radial basis functions. The field has singularities (degenerate points). — [Purdue course notes](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/urban/sg_2011_course/umc_SG11_02_urban_layouts.pdf); [Project page](https://www2.cs.uh.edu/~chengu/Publications/streetModeling/street_modeling.html)
- Chen et al. basis fields (Eq. 1-3): grid element T = l·[[cos2θ, sin2θ],[sin2θ, −cos2θ]] (constant direction θ); radial element T = [[y²−x², −2xy],[−2xy, −(y²−x²)]] with x, y relative to the centre (major hyperstreamlines are circles, minor ones emanate from the centre); blended as T(p) = Σ e^(−d‖p−pᵢ‖²) Tᵢ(p) with decay constant d. The paper gives no value for d. — [Chen et al. 2008 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~zhange/images/street_sig08.pdf)
- Height-field handling in Chen et al.: the tensor's minor eigenvector is aligned with the height gradient, using θ = arctan((∂H/∂y)/(∂H/∂x)) + π/2 and tensor magnitude R = √((∂H/∂x)² + (∂H/∂y)²). Roads therefore run perpendicular to the steepest slope, i.e. along contours. The paper does not describe any explicit contour-extraction step. — [Chen et al. 2008 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~zhange/images/street_sig08.pdf)
- Water boundaries become polylines with a regular element at each segment start, major eigenvector along the segment, so streets run parallel to shorelines. — [Chen et al. 2008 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~zhange/images/street_sig08.pdf)
- Tracing in Chen et al.: adaptive Runge-Kutta (Cash-Karp); sign ambiguity resolved by choosing the eigenvector with Ev·Vpre ≥ 0 continuity (paper's wording: Ev · Vpre ≤ 0 after flipping); stops at domain boundary, degenerate point, return to origin, max length, or violation of dsep; continues dlookahead to find intersections. Major and minor streamlines are traced alternately with each new seed at distance dsep along the previous streamline. dsep is user-specified and modulated by a road-density map. Seed priority ω = e^(−db) + e^(−ds) + e^(−dp) (distance to water, degenerate points, population centres). The domain is a 512×512 grid. No numeric dsep or dlookahead is reported. — [Chen et al. 2008 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~zhange/images/street_sig08.pdf)
- Street graph in Chen et al.: nodes are major/minor intersections, edges join consecutive intersections, roads carry width and type attributes; tracing can cross narrow water to form bridges depending on bridge length and angle to coast. Rotation fields R1/R2/R3 (one example R2 in [0°, 20°]) and Perlin noise mapped to [−π/2, π/2] break regularity. — [Chen et al. 2008 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~zhange/images/street_sig08.pdf)
- Chen et al. reports a reasonably complex city in about five minutes of modeling, plus 30-60 minutes of refinement. Source code is C++. — [Project page](https://www2.cs.uh.edu/~chengu/Publications/streetModeling/street_modeling.html)
- Galin et al. 2010 finds roads as weighted anisotropic shortest paths between two points with parameterised terrain cost functions, and models tunnels and bridges with a few geometric parameters. — [Purdue course notes](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/urban/sg_2011_course/umc_SG11_02_urban_layouts.pdf)
- Lechner et al. (agent-based) accept a connector road only if the detour exceeds a constant k times the Manhattan distance to the target; lower k gives more loops, higher k more dead ends. Their extenders avoid routes with large elevation change and value land near water and slightly above average elevation more highly. — [arXiv 2507.18899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18899)
- Aliaga et al. 2008 synthesise street/parcel layouts from example GIS data; Lipp et al. 2011 give layer-based editing of street topology. — [Purdue course notes](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/urban/sg_2011_course/umc_SG11_02_urban_layouts.pdf)

### Inferences
- Recipe for contour-following roads given a scalar field H(x,y) (for the project: any field such as a cymatic/Chladni amplitude): (1) compute ∇H; (2) set θ = atan2(∂H/∂y, ∂H/∂x) + π/2; (3) build T = R·[[cos2θ, sin2θ],[sin2θ, −cos2θ]] per cell with R = |∇H| (or clamp/normalise so flat areas do not vanish); (4) blend with grid/radial basis fields using the Gaussian decay so flat regions still get a street pattern; (5) trace major hyperstreamlines (they follow contours, "ring roads") and minor ones (they run up/down slope, "connectors") with dsep spacing.
- Because the field is built from a gradient, degenerate points sit at peaks, pits and saddles of H. Around a peak or pit the major streamlines become concentric rings and the minor ones become radial spokes, which is the same topology as Palmanova/Karlsruhe-style radial towns (see Section 4). A scalar field with a central extremum therefore naturally yields a radial/ring city.
- Evenly spaced streamlines (dsep) are not the same as fixed-interval contour lines (constant Δh). On a constant slope s the two agree if dsep ≈ Δh/s; on steep terrain contours bunch up, and tracing with dsep avoids that. For explicit, quantised terraces, marching-squares isolines plus radial connectors would also work, but this is my suggestion and not from a source.
- A slope-based road class rule is a straightforward add-on: in a Galin-style or Chen-style tracer, terminate or reroute a street if |∇H| exceeds a max grade threshold. No source gave a numeric grade threshold (see Gaps).
- Parish & Müller-style L-systems are best used for hierarchy (highway vs street, local pattern choice by population density) and for lot/building generation; they are weaker than tensor fields at honouring a global scalar field's geometry.

### Gaps
- No numeric dsep, dlookahead, dtest or decay-constant d values were found in the Chen paper text I could fetch.
- Parish & Müller's own rule parameters (branching angles, segment lengths, highway/street lengths) were not found in the sources fetched; only the high-level description is cited.
- No source for maximum road grade or contour-to-road alignment tolerances.

## 2. Real-world urban metrics

### Takeaway
Streets should take at least ~30% of city land (UN-Habitat recommends 30-35% streets, 15-20% open space, 45-50% total public space), with at least ~18 km of street per km² and at least 150 people/ha for sustainable neighbourhoods. Typical standard city blocks are ~80×274 m (Manhattan) to ~100×200 m (Chicago, Melbourne); floor-to-floor heights are roughly 2.4-3.7 m residential and 3.0-4.9 m office/retail.

### Cited Findings
- Streets: "at least 30 per cent of the land" for streets; at least 18 km of street per km². Adjacent streets about 111 m apart; arterials 800-1,000 m apart. — [UN-Habitat, A New Strategy of Sustainable Neighbourhood Planning: Five Principles](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/download-manager-files/A%20New%20Strategy%20of%20Sustainable%20Neighbourhood%20Planning%20Five%20principles.pdf)
- Density: at least 15,000 people/km² (150 people/ha). — same UN-Habitat document
- Land-use mix: at least 40% of floor space for economic use; recommended 40-60% economic, 30-50% residential, ~10% public services. — same UN-Habitat document
- Roads and parking take 20-30% of urban land and 40-60% of commercial-centre land (research cited by UN-Habitat). — same UN-Habitat document
- Public space shares recommended by UN-Habitat Global Urban Observatories Unit researchers: streets 30-35%, open space (parks, plazas) 15-20%, total public space 45-50%. Joan Clos: "If less than 30 percent of the land of the city is dedicated to the street pattern, it's a huge error." — [Next City](https://nextcity.org/urbanist-news/how-much-public-space-does-a-city-need-UN-Habitat-joan-clos-50-percent)
- Block sizes: Manhattan standard block about 264×900 ft (80×274 m); Chicago typical block 330×660 ft (100×200 m); Melbourne 330×660 ft (100×200 m), made by splitting square blocks with a narrow street. — [Wikipedia, City block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_block)
- Floor-to-floor heights: single-family and small residential 8-10 ft (2.4-3.0 m); apartment buildings 9-12 ft (2.7-3.7 m); low/mid-rise office 10-14 ft (3.0-4.3 m); commercial/retail 12-16 ft (3.7-4.9 m). Metric values are conversions. — [Flooring Clarity](https://www.flooringclarity.com/how-tall-is-a-floor/) (low-authority source, consistent with the task brief's 3 m / 3.5-4 m)
- Office space per person (France, AFNOR NF X35-102, gross incl. circulation): 10 m² individual office, 11 m² closed shared office, 15 m² open space; well-laid-out open space 6-8 m²; flex offices ~5 m² per active user; circulation and common areas 30-50% of total surface. — [Technis](https://www.technis.com/en/blog/how-many-m2-per-person-should-you-plan-for-your-offices)
- FAR examples: India typically about 1.3-3.25 (Mumbai 1.33, Bangalore 1.75 on 40-ft streets and 3.25 on 100-ft streets); Empire State Building (pre-1961 zoning) FAR 25. — [Wikipedia, Floor area ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_area_ratio)
- Setbacks, Barcelona Eixample: blocks have chamfered (octagonal) corners with streets widening at intersections; the planned open inner garden was in practice built up on all four sides. No numbers fetched. — [Wikipedia, Eixample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eixample)
- Existing-city density reference: Masdar City, 6 km², designed for 45,000-50,000 residents with 1,500 businesses and 60,000+ commuting workers; actual about 15,000 people (5,000 residents) by 2023. Its design used "short, narrow streets" and raised districts 7 m to separate pedestrians from vehicles. — [Wikipedia, Masdar City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masdar_City)

### Inferences
- Implementable street budget: reserve 30-35% of developed land for streets, 15-20% for parks/plazas, leaving ~45-55% for buildable parcels. Check the generator's output against these (area of road polygons / total area).
- UN-Habitat street spacing of ~111 m is consistent with the real block widths above (80-100 m wide plus street width). A plausible block-size sampler is width 80-110 m, length 100-275 m, with arterials every 800-1,000 m.
- Density sanity band: ≥150 people/ha is a "sustainable" floor; Masdar's planned density works out to 45,000-50,000 / 600 ha = 75-83 people/ha, so it is much lower than the UN-Habitat floor, while The Line and Sky City (Section 3) are orders of magnitude higher. (Derived arithmetic.)
- Storey count from height: floors = floor(H / floor_to_floor), with 3.0 m residential, ~4.0 m office, ~4.5 m ground-floor retail as defaults. (Choice of defaults is mine, within the cited ranges.)
- Gross-area budget per person for generated towers: ~10-15 m² of gross office area per worker (AFNOR above); this gives a headcount for a tower from its total floor area. Residential m²/person was not sourced.

### Gaps
- No sourced FAR figures for typical small towns, suburbs, or dense downtowns outside India; Manhattan and Hong Kong current FAR values not found.
- No sourced residential floor area per person, building setback distances, street width classes (lane widths, sidewalk widths), or UN-Habitat intersection-density figure (the document does not state one).
- No sourced typical built-up site coverage or lot dimensions (frontage × depth).
- Barcelona Eixample block (commonly cited as ~113 m square with ~20 m streets) could not be confirmed from the fetched page.

## 3. Structural plausibility: slenderness, height limits, gravity scaling, megastructures

### Takeaway
Engineers call a tower "slender" beyond about 1:10-1:12 height-to-width; the real extremes are 432 Park Avenue at about 1:15 and Steinway Tower (111 W 57th) at about 1:24 (435 m tall). Height is limited by wind and dynamic behaviour on Earth, but with gravity as the only varied parameter the pure self-weight compression limit h = σ/(ρg) scales as 1/g (granite: ~3.4 km on Earth, ~9 km Mars, ~21 km Moon). Proposed megastructures (X-Seed 4000, Sky City 1000, The Line) all use very low slenderness (1:1.5 to 1:2.5 for the main cross-section) or are not built.

### Cited Findings
- Slenderness threshold: engineers treat a tower as slender when its ratio exceeds roughly 1:10 to 1:12; 432 Park Avenue is 1:15; 111 West 57th Street is 1:24. — [Architectural Record](https://www.architecturalrecord.com/articles/15106-a-cliff-dwellers-guide-to-tall-skinny-buildings)
- Steinway Tower (111 W 57th St): 1,428 ft (435 m), width-to-height ratio approximately 1:24; 432 Park Avenue 1,396 ft; ONE57 1,005 ft. — [Architizer](https://architizer.com/blog/inspiration/industry/worlds-thinnest-skyscraper-facts/)
- Burj Khalifa: 828 m architectural height (829.8 m tip), 163 floors (page also lists 154 plus 9 maintenance), buttressed central core with three wings (Y plan), reinforced concrete with a steel spire, 27 spiral setbacks to reduce vortex-shedding wind loads, total sway at the top about 1.5 m, about 330,000 m³ of concrete. Foundation calculation assumed 30 MPa concrete; 192 piles 1.5 m diameter × 43 m long. — [Wikipedia, Burj Khalifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burj_Khalifa) (the page gives no slenderness ratio; the "12 m" foundation dimension there should not be used to compute one)
- Compressive-strength limit on height: Height_max = yield strength / (density × g). Granite ~100 MPa, ~3,000 kg/m³ gives about 3 km on Earth (page uses g = 10); my recalculation from the page's formula: Earth ~3.4 km, Mars (g 3.7) ~9 km, Moon (g 1.62) ~21 km, Ceres (g 0.27) ~120 km. Observed tallest mountains exceed the simple formula (Everest 10 km, Olympus Mons 21 km), and the page gives no explanation or safety factor. — [Jay Maron, Mountains and the roundness of solar system objects](https://jaymaron.com/oom/mountains.html)
- Material strengths: normal concrete 17-70 MPa; UHPC above 150 MPa; steel 250-1,500 MPa. — [Wikipedia, Compressive strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressive_strength)
- X-Seed 4000 (Taisei Corporation, 1995, Tokyo): 4,000 m tall, 800 floors, sea-based platform 6 km wide, 500,000-1,000,000 people, over 3 million tonnes of steel, Mount Fuji-inspired shape; active systems needed for pressure and weather, maglev transit; status proposed only. — [Wikipedia, X-Seed 4000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-Seed_4000)
- Sky City 1000: 1,000 m tall, 400 m base width, 196 floors, 35,000-36,000 residents plus 100,000 workers, 8 km² floor area, 14 stacked dish-shaped "Space Plateaus", triple-decker high-speed elevators; never built. — [Wikipedia, Sky City 1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_City_1000)
- The Line (NEOM): 170 km long, 200 m wide, 500 m tall, up to 9 million residents, projected average density of 260,000 per km² (two mirrored buildings with open space between). Phase 1 cut back in 2024 reports (central 5 km segment targeted 2030, full project 2045), PIF suspended construction in September 2025, and in May 2026 Semafor reported work delayed until at least after 2030. A Vienna Complexity Science Hub study found average distances between residents of about 57 km in a linear city versus 2.9 km in a circular city of equal area; 1.2% of residents within walking distance vs 24%. Critics said it relies on technology that does not exist and that some concept images would be dangerous. — [Wikipedia, The Line (Neom)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Line_(Neom))
- Arcosanti (Soleri): 25 acres (10 ha) within a 4,060-acre preserve at 1,138 m elevation; plan population 1,500 (1969) then 5,000 (1975-77 revision); actual population typically 50-150; 13 major structures, some several stories; visitor centre 5 stories. Soleri coined "arcology" (architecture + ecology) in 1969 to mean compact, high-density, mixed-use 3-D cities with near-elimination of private transport. — [Wikipedia, Arcosanti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcosanti); [Wikipedia, Arcology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcology)
- Other proposals with numbers: Fuller's Old Man River's City, 1,000 ft dome, up to 125,000 residents; Dubai "Ziggurat Pyramid", 1,200 m, 300+ floors, 2.3 km² footprint, nearly 1 million residents (low-authority source). — [Wikipedia, Arcology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcology); [Goff substack](https://goff.substack.com/p/october-10-2023-arcologies)

### Inferences
- Enforceable slenderness rule for tall towers (height H over minimum plan dimension W): H/W ≤ 7-8 free for ordinary buildings; 8-12 allowed with a visible structural system (core + outriggers/belt trusses, or setbacks like Burj Khalifa); 12-15 only for a small fraction of residential "pencil" towers (432 Park ≈ 15); hard cap ≈ 24 (Steinway). The 7-8 lower band is my assumption; the cited threshold is only "slender above 10-12".
- Gravity scaling (arithmetic from the cited formula, concrete 40 MPa / 2,400 kg/m³; steel 250 MPa / 7,850 kg/m³; no safety factor): concrete column limit ≈ 1,700 m Earth, ≈ 4,500 m Mars (3.71 m/s²), ≈ 10,300 m Moon; steel ≈ 3,250 m / 8,600 m / 19,700 m. With a safety factor of 3 (my choice), concrete becomes ≈ 570 m / 1,500 m / 3,400 m. Rule for the engine: h_max(g) = h_max(g_earth) · (9.81 / g) · (1 / SF).
- The prismatic-column formula overstates what a real building can do, since stress in real columns is set by the weight of the whole structure (floors, contents, cladding) carried by only a fraction φ of plan area. A more usable form is h_max ≈ σ_allow · φ / (ρ_eff · g), where ρ_eff is the building's average effective density (mostly air, so far lower than material density) and σ_allow = σ_c / SF. Choices of φ and ρ_eff are free parameters and were not sourced.
- A tapered column (constant stress) has base-to-top area ratio exp(ρ g h / σ), so at h = σ/(ρg) it is e ≈ 2.7×. This explains why proposals at extreme heights (X-Seed 4000, 4,000 m tall, 6 km base, aspect 1:1.5) are cone/mountain-shaped. The scaling (not a sourced figure) means on a low-g world the same footprint-to-height taper allows proportionally taller structures.
- Derived ratios for the proposals: Sky City 1000 is 1,000/400 = 2.5:1; X-Seed 4000 is 4,000/6,000 ≈ 1:1.5 (i.e. wider than tall); The Line cross-section is 500/200 = 2.5:1. Average gross height per floor is ≈ 5.1 m for Sky City (1,000 m / 196) and 5.0 m for X-Seed (4,000 m / 800), well above the 3-4 m typical floor-to-floor, which implies large mechanical/sky-lobby/atrium allowances in megastructures. The Line density: 9,000,000 / (170 km × 0.2 km = 34 km²) ≈ 265,000/km², consistent with the cited 260,000/km².
- Sky City 1000 per-person gross area ≈ 8 km² / (35,000 residents + 100,000 workers) ≈ 59 m²; counting only residents is ≈ 228 m² each. (Derived.)
- Wind is the normal governing load on Earth (Burj Khalifa's setbacks exist to disrupt vortex shedding), so on thin-atmosphere worlds (Moon, Mars) the compression and buckling limit becomes dominant and tall slender structures become more feasible. Atmosphere data was not researched here.
- Euler/Greenhill self-buckling of a free-standing column scales as g^(−1/3), a standard result I did not source here; it would limit very slender masts below the pure crushing limit (suggested check, flagged as unverified).
- Megastructure plausibility rules that follow: keep daily-life travel inside a 15-minute walk (The Line's linear form fails this; circular arrangement gives 24% within walking distance vs 1.2%); arcology-scale populations should be ≥ 1,000s/ha only when elevators/vertical transport are provided (Sky City's triple-deck lifts); and treat built reality as typically far below the plan (Arcosanti 50-150 residents vs 5,000 planned; Masdar 5,000 vs 45,000-50,000).

### Gaps
- No CTBUH-published slenderness distribution or database figures; CTBUH pages were not retrieved and the Emerald paper on slenderness was blocked by an anti-bot page.
- No sourced wind-sway limit (e.g. H/500 drift) or building-period rules; nothing on outrigger spacing or core-to-plan area ratios.
- No source found for Soleri's large arcology designs (Babel IIB, Hexahedron, Asimov, Mesa City) dimensions or populations; the fetched pages did not include them. Commonly quoted numbers could not be verified.
- No source on how real structures' effective density and structural-area fraction vary with height; the gravity-scaling formula above therefore relies on free parameters.
- No source on atmosphere/wind loads on other worlds, seismic design, or foundation limits by gravity.

## 4. Radial and concentric planned cities in history

### Takeaway
Real radial plans combine one dominant centre, concentric ring roads, and straight spokes, but on the ground the structure is usually only partial (fan or wheel segments, axes cut by water or topography) and the plan is much smaller than modern city scales. Karlsruhe (32 spokes from a palace tower, 1715), Palmanova (9-bastion star with three 9-sided rings, 1593), Nahalal (80 equal parcels radiating from a hub, 1921), Canberra (hexagonal/octagonal rings with axes tied to landmarks) and L'Enfant's Washington (grid overlaid with diagonal avenues) give the pattern parameters.

### Cited Findings
- Palmanova (founded 7 October 1593): nine bastions with nine ravelins, nine lunettes and eighteen cavaliers; three large guarded gates; three nine-sided ring roads meeting main radiating streets; a six-sided central square; first circle took 30 years to build (second phase 1658-1690, outer line 1806-1813); the first circle's total circumference is given as 7 km. The settlement was meant for "self-sustaining merchants, craftsmen, and farmers". The page gives no diameter or population. — [Wikipedia, Palmanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmanova); layout (radial from a central hexagonal square, concentric rings crossed by straight avenues, star-shaped double fortification) also in [ESA, Earth from Space: Star cities](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2024/12/Earth_from_Space_Star_cities)
- Karlsruhe (founded 17 June 1715): 32 streets radiate from the palace tower like a fan; the oldest city centre is in the quadrant south of the palace bounded by nine radial streets; the palace's central part runs east-west with wings angled at 45°; the "Karlsruhe metric" assumes travel only along radial streets and circular avenues. Current city: 173.46 km², 16.8 km N-S by 19.3 km E-W. — [Wikipedia, Karlsruhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlsruhe)
- Nahalal (Richard Kauffmann, 1921): slightly oval round plan like a spoke wheel; public buildings (school, administration, services, warehouses) at the hub; roads divide it into eight equal sectors; inner ring houses non-farming families (craftsmen, teachers); an outer ring street has farmers' homesteads on its outer side; concentric gardens and fields beyond; originally 80 equal parcels (75 for members, 5 for the agricultural school); total area 8.5 km². — [Wikipedia, Nahalal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahalal)
- Canberra (Walter Burley Griffin plan): concentric hexagonal and octagonal streets from several radii; wheel-and-spoke main roads near Lake Burley Griffin; Land Axis from Capital Hill north-north-east across the lake's central basin to the Australian War Memorial and Mount Ainslie, its south-west end towards Bimberi Peak about 52 km away; Water Axis perpendicular to it; Municipal Axis parallel to the water axis (now Constitution Avenue); Parliamentary Triangle enclosed by Commonwealth and Kings Avenues and the Municipal Axis. Griffin tied components to topographical landmarks, and the outer suburbs built later are not geometric. ACT 2,358 km², Canberra built-up area population 452,670 (2021 census). — [Wikipedia, Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canberra)
- L'Enfant plan (Washington): lettered east-west and numbered north-south grid crossed by broad diagonal "grand avenues" (named for states; Pennsylvania Avenue links the President's House and the Capitol); intersections form circles and rectangular plazas that became open space; a 160-ft-wide avenue about a mile long; a 400-ft-wide garden-lined grand avenue (the Mall) about a mile long; 15 state squares; the plan was bounded by the Potomac, the Eastern Branch, the Fall Line escarpment and Rock Creek. — [Wikipedia, L'Enfant Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%27Enfant_Plan)
- Masdar City (planned 2006 era): 6 km², designed for 45,000-50,000 residents, districts raised 7 m above ground to separate pedestrians and vehicles, wind towers and a planned underground personal rapid transit that was cut back to a pilot because the undercroft was too costly. — [Wikipedia, Masdar City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masdar_City)
- Linear versus radial: Vienna Complexity Science Hub found average inter-resident distance 57 km in a linear city versus 2.9 km in a circular city of equal area. — [Wikipedia, The Line (Neom)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Line_(Neom))

### Inferences
- How ring/radial structure works in practice (my synthesis of the cited cases): (a) one civic/defensive centre with a distinct plaza; (b) a small number of spokes (Palmanova 3 gates / 9 bastions, Nahalal 8 sectors, Karlsruhe 32 spokes within a fan roughly a half-circle); (c) 2-3 concentric rings (Palmanova's three); (d) rings subdivide spokes into wedge-shaped parcels, so parcel width grows with radius, which means generators must either vary the spoke count per ring (insert new radial streets at larger radii) or accept wedge-shaped blocks; (e) real plans are usually truncated by water or terrain (Karlsruhe is a fan, not a full circle; Canberra's rings are hexagonal/octagonal and bent to lake and hills).
- Parcel count arithmetic: Nahalal's 8.5 km² / 80 parcels is about 10.6 ha gross per parcel (includes village, school and roads); a 8-sector wheel therefore has about 10 parcels per sector.
- Parameter set for a radial generator: spokes N_s (8 for Nahalal, 9 or 18 for a Palmanova-style, 32 for Karlsruhe), rings N_r = 2-3 for small towns, polygon sides (9 for Palmanova; 6 and 8 for Canberra) versus true circles; add diagonal avenues over a grid (L'Enfant) for hybrid plans; hierarchical widths with a 160-ft (≈49 m) avenue and 400-ft (≈122 m) mall as upper-end boulevard values from L'Enfant.
- A radial plan from a scalar field: tensor-field radial basis elements (Section 1) produce circles plus spokes without hand-placing rings. A field with several local extrema yields several radial nuclei joined by gridded or contour-following streets, which resembles Canberra's multiple-centre structure.
- A radial form is a plausible compromise between the density of a circle and the ease of a grid: the cited circular-versus-linear comparison shows radius/compactness mostly drives walkability (24% within walking distance in the circular case).
